# Why the taxonomy uses 5W1H

Every concept in the Aecoware Open Taxonomy has one or two of six tags: **Who, What, Where, When, Why and How**. The first tag is the primary tag. This page tells why we use the six questions, how an agent can use them to route a question, and where the questions come from.

## The six tags

| Tag | It tells | Examples | Concepts | Primary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who | which person, company or authority does or controls something | Authority having jurisdiction (`aeco-00114`), Project inspector (`aeco-01039`) | 82 | 57 |
| What | which thing, product or content is part of the work | Beam (`aeco-01008`), Ceiling (`aeco-00604`) | 593 | 579 |
| Where | the place, the site or the jurisdiction and its rules | Level (`aeco-00527`), Fire compartment (`aeco-00532`) | 210 | 161 |
| When | the time, the phase or the order of an occurrence | Notice to proceed (`aeco-00047`), Substantial completion (`aeco-00048`) | 91 | 53 |
| Why | the reason, the requirement or the purpose | Owner's project requirements (`aeco-00368`), Statutory requirement (`aeco-00377`) | 90 | 49 |
| How | the method, the process or the means | Request inspection (`aeco-00993`), Close RFI (`aeco-00977`) | 253 | 174 |

Counts are for v0.1.0. "Concepts" counts every concept with the tag. "Primary" counts the concepts where it is the first tag. The tags are in `src/tags.ttl`. Each concept links them with `aeco:tag`, and the first one also with `aeco:primaryTag`.

## Why six questions

- **People already ask in these six questions.** A question on site is a mix of them: who signs, what goes in, where, by when, why and how. Daily reports, incident reports and RFIs already answer them.
- **A language model reads a request in the same parts.** Linguists call them [semantic roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_role_labeling): who acts, on what, in which place, at which time, for which reason and in which manner. A model can sort a question into the six with a short instruction.
- **Six is a small number.** Anyone can apply the tags, check them and argue about them. A tagging scheme that only experts can apply does not get checked.
- **The tags show what is missing.** In v0.1.0, What is the primary tag of 579 concepts (54%). Why is the primary tag of 49 (under 5%). The industry has many words for things and few for reasons. But the reasons, which are the requirements, the rules and the decisions, are where an agent must be right.

## Why tags and not the tree

The first design used the six questions as the six roots of the tree. It did not work. Many concepts answer two questions. A fire inspection is part of the rules of the jurisdiction (Where) and a step in the order of work (When). In v0.1.0, 246 concepts (23%) have two tags. A tree gives each concept one place, so it had to drop the second question for each of them.

Now each structure does one job:

- **The tree tells what a concept is.** It follows the groups of ISO 12006-2: built assets, spaces, elements, work results, processes, resources and properties.
- **The tags tell which question a concept answers.** One concept can answer two.

The most frequent pairs are How and What (46 concepts), How and Where (39) and What and Why (30).

## How an agent can route a question

Take this question from a site: *"Can we close up the ceiling on level 3 before the fire inspection?"*

| Tag | Part of the question | Concepts to look at |
|---|---|---|
| Who | who must approve | Authority having jurisdiction (`aeco-00114`), Fire marshal (`aeco-00116`) |
| What | what is closed up, and what must be inspected first | Ceiling (`aeco-00604`), Firestopping (`aeco-00734`) |
| Where | the location, and the rules that apply there | Level (`aeco-00527`), Fire code (`aeco-00310`) |
| When | what comes before what | Fire inspection (`aeco-00340`) |
| Why | the reason the inspection is necessary | Statutory requirement (`aeco-00377`), Fire-resistance rating (`aeco-00884`) |
| How | the steps that let the work continue | Request inspection (`aeco-00993`), Record inspection result (`aeco-00994`) |

An agent can use the tags in four steps:

1. **Sort the question.** Find which of the six questions each part of the question answers.
2. **Narrow, then search.** Look only at the concepts with the matching tag. A plain word then matches fewer wrong concepts.
3. **Start with the primary tag.** It tells where to look first. A second tag tells where else to look. Fire inspection is Where first and When second, so a scheduling question finds it too.
4. **Show what is missing.** The agent can show which of the six questions its context answers. If a necessary answer is missing, the agent can make an assumption and log it, ask a person, or stop. Which one depends on what is at risk.

A record of what an agent did can use the same six fields: who did what, where, when, why and how.

## What the tags do not do

- **They do not give permission.** A tag helps an agent find concepts. It does not let an agent act. Each task needs its own required context and its own approvals.
- **They are a judgement.** Two reviewers can tag a concept differently. To propose a change to a tag, see [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md).
- **There is no "How much".** Some quality methods add it as a seventh question (5W2H). In this taxonomy, cost and quantity are properties, in the Properties branch (`aeco-00009`).

## Where the six questions come from

The six questions are old. They are a check that a description of an event is complete.

- **Aristotle**, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book III, chapter 1 (about 1111a). To judge an action, you must know who acts, what they do, what the act affects, with what instrument, for what result and in what manner. [Text on Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicomachean_Ethics_(Chase)/Book_Three)
- **Hermagoras of Temnos**, a Greek teacher of rhetoric. The "seven circumstances" of a case are credited to him: who, what, when, where, why, in what way and by what means. His work is known only through later writers, such as Cicero and Quintilian. [Hermagoras of Temnos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermagoras_of_Temnos)
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Summa Theologiae*, I-II, question 7, article 3. He quotes a Latin verse of the seven circumstances, which he credits to Cicero: *"Quis, quid, ubi, quibus auxiliis, cur, quomodo, quando"*. In English: who, what, where, by what means, why, how, when. [Text at CCEL](https://ccel.org/ccel/aquinas/summa.FS_Q7_A3.html)
- **Rudyard Kipling**, *Just So Stories* (1902). The poem after "The Elephant's Child" begins:

  > I keep six honest serving-men\
  > (They taught me all I knew);\
  > Their names are What and Why and When\
  > And How and Where and Who.

  Kipling did not invent the list. He gave it the form that most people remember. The "Kipling 5W1H" view in the explorer has its name from this poem. [The Kipling Society](https://www.kiplingsociety.co.uk/?p=7688)
- **Journalism.** By 1917, the "five Ws" were taught in high-school journalism classes in the United States as the parts of a news lead. [Five Ws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Ws)
- **Information systems.** John Zachman's "A Framework for Information Systems Architecture" (IBM Systems Journal, 1987) describes an enterprise in columns. The columns came to have the names of the six questions: What, How, Where, Who, When and Why. [Zachman Framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zachman_Framework)

For more than two thousand years, people have used the six questions to make sure that a description of an event is complete. This taxonomy uses them to send a question to the correct concepts.
